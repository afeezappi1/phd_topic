Parent–child ties

Family and community

Slum-community influences affect attendance and wellbeing; fieldwork can get costly.

Gender and opportunity

Gender equity is a stated mission, so gender attitudes and aspirations are relevant and sensitive.

Trauma-sensitive learning

A distinctive angle. Avoid trauma screening of children; study climate, teachers and belonging instead.

Topic A · School belonging, family adversity and engagement
Refined title
School Belonging as a Protective Pathway from Family Adversity to Academic Engagement among Adolescents in Slum-Serving NGO Schools: Roles of Teacher Support and Locus of Control
Rationale
Belonging is linked to engagement, but evidence from Indian slum-serving schools needs checking. Parikrma reports high attendance, so belonging may be a mechanism worth testing.
Problem
It is unclear how and for whom school belonging reduces the engagement cost of family adversity.
Gap (verify)
Possible scarcity of mediation or moderation studies in Indian NGO schools. Do not claim until searched.
Objectives
Test adversity → belonging → engagement; test teacher support and locus of control as moderators; explore student views qualitatively.
Questions
Does belonging mediate adversity and engagement? Does teacher support strengthen the path? Does it differ by gender or branch?
Hypotheses
H1 adversity negatively predicts belonging. H2 belonging predicts engagement. H3 belonging mediates. H4 teacher support moderates the adversity–belonging path.
Variables
IV: family adversity. Mediator: belonging. DV: engagement. Moderators: teacher support, locus of control. Controls: grade, gender, branch.
Population, sample
Grades 8–12 across all branches; stratified by branch and grade. Target about 350–400 (mediation power studies suggest a few hundred; check with G*Power or Monte Carlo, allow about 15% loss). School is a covariate because there are too few clusters for multilevel modelling.
Tools
Psychological Sense of School Membership (Goodenow); Student Engagement Instrument; teacher support subscale of CASSS; Nowicki–Strickland child locus of control scale. Adversity via a family risk index from school records plus a daily-stressors checklist, with no trauma-exposure screening.
Design
Cross-sectional survey, then 15–20 explanatory interviews (sequential mixed methods). A short follow-up wave is optional.
Procedure
Group administration in class time, 30–40 minutes, with a non-supervisory administrator reading items aloud in the child’s language.
Analysis
Reliability, CFA of scales, regression with PROCESS or SEM mediation and moderation, thematic analysis of interviews.
Ethics
University ethics approval, leadership permission, parental consent and child assent, anonymous codes, a distress-referral protocol, voluntary participation without academic consequences, and compliance with India’s data-protection rules for children’s data (confirm).
Workload
About 6 weeks of data collection across branches. Feasible part-time. Feasibility: high.
Topic B · Teacher trauma-informed attitudes, compassion fatigue and wellbeing
Refined title
Trauma-Informed Attitudes, Secondary Traumatic Stress and Wellbeing among Teachers in Trauma-Sensitive NGO Schools
Rationale
Trauma-sensitive teaching places emotional demands on staff. Teacher wellbeing also shapes children’s experience.
Problem
It is unclear how teachers’ attitudes toward trauma-informed practice relate to their own stress and wellbeing.
Gap (verify)
Indian NGO-school evidence may be thin. Confirm through a systematic search.
Objectives
Describe attitudes and wellbeing; test links between them; test teacher self-efficacy and organisational support as moderators.
Questions
Do more trauma-informed attitudes go with higher or lower compassion fatigue? Does support buffer the link?
Hypotheses
H1 secondary traumatic stress predicts burnout. H2 self-efficacy buffers it. H3 perceived support relates to wellbeing.
Variables
IV: attitudes, secondary stress. DV: burnout, wellbeing. Moderators: self-efficacy, support.
Population, sample
Census of teachers in all branches. Need at least 100–120 (about 92 for a medium effect with five predictors). Confirm how many teachers exist; if too few, add non-teaching staff.
Tools
ARTIC scale; ProQOL-5; Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (free alternative to the licensed MBI-Educators); Teachers’ Sense of Efficacy Scale; WHO-5.
Design
Cross-sectional survey plus 10–12 interviews.
Procedure
Online or paper survey, anonymous, administered by someone who is not their line manager.
Analysis
Correlation, hierarchical regression, moderation, thematic analysis.
Ethics
Staff may feel pressured as you are a colleague, so use anonymity, no identifiers and no reporting of results by individual.
Workload
About 3–4 weeks. Feasibility: very high, but sample-size risk.
Topic C · Imposter feelings and belonging in first-generation learners
Refined title
Imposter Feelings, Sense of Belonging and Locus of Control among First-Generation Learners Transitioning from NGO Schooling to College and Work
Rationale
Moving from a supportive NGO school to unfamiliar institutions can trigger self-doubt. It builds on your MSc scales and expertise.
Problem
Little may be known about how Indian slum-origin first-generation students experience imposter feelings.
Gap (verify)
Check literature on first-generation students in India.
Objectives
Measure imposter feelings and belonging; test locus of control as a predictor; understand lived transition experiences.
Questions
Which factors predict imposter feelings? How do learners describe the transition?
Hypotheses
H1 external locus of control predicts higher imposter feelings. H2 belonging and social support predict lower.
Variables
DV: imposter feelings. IV: locus of control, belonging, social support. Control: gender, course type.
Population, sample
Junior-college students, recent alumni in college or work. Target at least 120; alumni need tracing through the alumni network.
Tools
Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale; Rotter or Levenson locus of control; Psychological Sense of School Membership (adapted); MSPSS.
Design
Sequential mixed methods: survey, then 12–15 interviews. An optional 6-month follow-up adds depth.
Procedure
Online survey for alumni, in-person group sessions for current students.
Analysis
Regression, group comparisons, thematic analysis.
Ethics
Alumni contact permission, privacy, voluntary participation, and support referral if distress emerges.
Workload
About 6–8 weeks (alumni response is slow). Feasibility: moderate.

6My recommendation

Topic A: School belonging, family adversity and engagement

Large student poolShort validated scalesAll 4–5 branchesMixed methodsClear PhD model
Why it fits
  • Manageable: one survey wave in class time, then a small interview set.
  • Interesting: it tests a mechanism and who benefits, not just a correlation. Locus of control continues your MSc work.
  • PhD-level: a survey study, a qualitative study and an optional follow-up wave make three linked chapters.
  • Useful: results can show Parikrma which belonging practices matter.
Challenges
  • Parental consent and child assent across branches.
  • Your dual role as an employee; use a non-supervisory administrator.
  • Language: scales need Kannada or Hindi translation and back-translation.
  • Measuring adversity without harming children.
Backup: Topic B can serve as a pilot or fallback if child-consent approval is slow.
